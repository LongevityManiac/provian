# The Exponential Currency

## Why Parity Minting Is the Best Monetary Model for an Exponential Economy

October 2026 · Provian Technologies

---

## Summary

Output is becoming exponential. Compute, AI capability, and energy density are on curves that do not flatten on any timeline that matters for monetary design. Nearly every cryptocurrency built to date assumes the opposite: fixed supply, hard caps, halvings, scarcity as the entire value story.

That is a category error. You cannot denominate exponential output in deflationary money without either breaking incentives or destroying savings. Both failure modes are documented across a century of monetary history, and neither survives at scale.

Parity minting is the alternative: supply that grows as fast as verified output grows, and no faster, with every new unit going to whoever did the work. It is not a tokenomics tweak. It is the monetary model best fitted to an economy whose output curve bends upward.

PVT, the currency of the Provian network, implements it end to end. Supply is uncapped. Every unit is minted from a witnessed act of work, once, at the value its evidence supports. New units go to the contributor, never to the holder. Participants use burns for specific functions: they destroy PVT to be trusted, to speak in public, and to commit to one another, so that the currency keeps circulating and the economy stays open to whoever arrives next.

The serious competitor is not a scarcity coin. It is the dollar and its on-chain wrappers. The dollar is elastic, stablecoins settle at machine speed, and PVT is built to live alongside both. But a stablecoin moves money without making any, and a central bank can only print for a whole economy at once, delivering the new money to whoever stands nearest the issuer. Neither lets a unit come into existence because work happened and hands it to whoever did the work. That is the job parity minting exists to do.

It also changes who pays for the exponential itself. A currency finances only what its participants earn and accept in it, and today the build-out is paid for in dollars by people who earn in dollars. Parity minting changes what is possible as that boundary moves: supply accumulates across every participant whose work the network witnesses, and when something large is proposed, the money to commit to it already exists and is spread across the people and machines who earned it, rather than printed for everyone at once or allocated by whoever already held the capital.

No other project has built this. Bitcoin fixes supply. Ethereum and Solana pay new coins to whoever stakes what they already hold. Energy-denominated money, the successor to fiat that some now propose, is issued to whoever owns the generation. None of them mint against work. The question each of them answers is what to hold. The question this document answers is who receives: money as the instrument that records what participants are worth to each other, in a unit that keeps its meaning as the economy grows.

None of this is a small claim, and this document does not pretend it is. The people building the exponential say openly that money will have to change, and they name the problems this paper takes up, falling prices, elastic money that pays the holder first, output no ledger counts, without proposing a rule for issuance that answers them. A solution has to be as consequential as the problems. The path from a dollar-financed present to an economy that counts its own growth is not mapped here, and nobody can map it yet. What can be built now is the architecture that makes the destination reachable: a receipt for work, a unit minted from it, and a burn that keeps the economy open. That is what PVT is. Whether the world walks the path is decided by the participants who use it, not by anything written here.

## 1. What PVT Is

The rest of this document is an economic argument, and it needs three facts about Provian on the table first. The Provian Thesis sets each of them out in full; here they are in one breath.

When a person, an agent, or a device does useful work on the Provian network, the work leaves a receipt, a Provenance Envelope, owned by the one who did the work and carrying no private content. Every act that leaves such a receipt mints PVT, once, at the value its evidence supports, and nothing else creates it: no pre-mine, no issuance outside the settlement of witnessed work. In between, PVT is simply money. A participant pays it to another for a job, locks it in escrow against delivery, or accepts it for work, and a plain transfer is never gated and never burns. What it buys grows with who accepts it: inside the network first, and beyond it as more of the world's work leaves receipts. And PVT leaves the world the way it entered, one act at a time. Participants burn it, permanently and on chain, whenever they claim trust, take a public voice, or commit to one another, led by presenting their own history to someone who asks for proof. Earning is free. Reading is free. Claiming trust costs the claimant something real, in the only currency that work creates.

That is the whole machine: witnessed work mints, trust burns, and the currency in between is the measure of what the network's participants have done for one another. Everything that follows is about why that design holds up better than any alternative when output goes exponential.

## 2. The Exponential Output Problem

### The constraint

The productive economy is on an exponential curve in its fastest-growing segment. Compute capacity keeps doubling on a multi-year cadence. Inference cost per unit of capability is falling steeply. Energy per computation is falling. Output per unit of labor is accelerating wherever digital tooling reaches.

Aggregate real growth in developed economies still averages two to three percent a year. In the compute-adjacent economy it is far higher, and that segment is growing as a share of the whole. Any monetary design that ignores this is designing for a world that is ending.

### Why fixed supply breaks

Take an economy growing ten percent a year with a fixed money supply.

Year zero: one hundred currency units, one hundred units of goods. One unit buys one good.

Year one: one hundred ten units of goods. Still one hundred currency units. One unit now buys 1.1 goods.

That is deflation, arrived at arithmetically rather than by policy. The currency appreciates by doing nothing, and the faster the economy grows, the faster it appreciates.

### The behavioral catastrophe

Deflation breaks incentives at every level simultaneously.

Investment freezes. If cash buys more tomorrow than today, the rational move is to wait. Every actor reasons identically. Capital formation stops.

Hoarding wins. Holding becomes the dominant strategy. Risk-taking is punished relative to sitting still.

Lending becomes toxic. Every loan becomes an appreciation transfer from borrower to lender. The borrower is underwater on day one, before any interest. Credit markets contract.

This is not theory. It is the Great Depression, Japan's lost decade, and every deflationary episode on record.

### Why inflation is also broken

The conventional fix, inflate faster than growth, produces its own failure.

Savings are punished. A lifetime of saved purchasing power erodes without the saver doing anything wrong.

Holders are grifted. Newly created value accrues to whoever held currency before the work was done, not to whoever did it.

Trust collapses. Issuance by decree means someone decides who receives the new money. That decision is political, and populations correctly perceive it as extraction.

Both failure modes share one root: the money supply is set by something other than what the economy actually produced.

## 3. Parity Minting

### The core idea

Grow the money supply at the rate verified output grows. If output grows ten percent, mint ten percent. Not two. Not twenty. Ten.

The consequence follows directly. Purchasing power per unit is not pushed up by scarcity or down by decree. Savers are not punished. Holders are not enriched for holding. The currency is neutral, and the measurement it carries stays meaningful.

### The load-bearing claim

New value created by work flows to whoever created it.

Return to the fixed-supply economy above. The ten additional goods in year one are claimed by whoever held currency in year zero. Workers created the value. Holders captured it.

Under parity minting, year one has one hundred ten goods and one hundred ten units, the ten new units minted to the people who did the work. Holders' purchasing power is unchanged. Contributors' wealth rises by exactly what they contributed.

This is the inversion. Not a gentler inflation, not a softer deflation, but a different allocation rule entirely. Scarcity pays the holder. Decree pays whoever the issuer favors. Parity minting pays the doer.

### Why the unit has to be tied to work

A supply rule needs an anchor, and the candidates are few.

Anchor to time, and you get Bitcoin: issuance on a schedule that knows nothing about what the economy produced.

Anchor to a committee's estimate of growth, and you get fiat: someone has to measure output, and whoever measures also decides, and the decision becomes the extraction the design was meant to prevent.

Anchor to energy, or to any physical input, and you get the holder again under a new name. A unit denominated in watts is either issued to whoever owns the generation, or it is not issued at all and is only a ruler that prices are quoted in. Either way it postpones the question of who receives the new units when output grows. Energy money is gold with a thermodynamic backing: it pays the owner of the joule, not the one who put it to use.

Anchor to the work itself, and you get parity by construction. The mint is not an estimate of output. It is the output, witnessed one act at a time. Nobody has to forecast growth; the growth shows up as receipts, and the receipts mint. This is the reason the Provenance Envelope and PVT are one design and not two. The receipt is what makes parity computable at all.

## 4. Why Not Just Use Dollars

The serious objection to all of this does not come from Bitcoin. Nobody proposes that the agent economy run on a fixed-supply coin. It comes from the dollar.

The argument runs like this. The dollar is not fixed; the Federal Reserve expands it whenever it judges the economy needs more. Dollar stablecoins such as USDC put that dollar on a public chain, where it settles in seconds, moves between agents without a bank, and carries the deepest liquidity in the digital economy. Agents already pay each other in it. If machine output outruns the money supply, the institution that issues the world's reserve currency can simply print more. Why build a new unit at all?

The objection deserves a real answer, because most of it is true. The dollar is elastic, stablecoins are excellent settlement rails, and nothing in Provian asks anyone to stop using either. An agent paid in dollars for a task a person handed it should be paid in dollars, and on Provian that payment is witnessed and mints like any other act. The question is narrower and harder: which unit should count the growth itself?

**A stablecoin moves money. It does not make any.** Every dollar stablecoin in circulation was issued against a dollar deposited with its issuer. Its supply grows when people want to hold dollars on chain and shrinks when they leave. It has no relationship to what its holders produce, and by design it cannot: a stablecoin that minted against work would no longer be a dollar.

**So the agent economy can only earn what the human economy pays into it.** An agent that does verified work does not cause a single unit to exist; it has to be paid by someone who already holds one. Two agents trading services with each other are passing back and forth dollars that some person deposited. The work between them adds output but not money. However much they produce, the money in their economy grows only when another deposit arrives from outside, and the unit ends up measuring how much humans chose to spend on agents rather than what agents actually did.

**Printing does not fix this, because a dollar is one unit for the whole economy.** Suppose the Federal Reserve resolved to expand the money supply as fast as machine output grows. It would first have to measure that output, which puts a committee back in the anchor role Part 3 rejects. And whatever rate it chose would apply to every dollar in every market the dollar denominates. Money printed at the pace of the compute economy is inflation for the grocer, the pensioner, and the vendor who never touches a model. A single unit cannot track the fastest-growing sector without debasing the slowest. Parity avoids this by construction: PVT mints only against the witnessed segment, and the rest of the economy is left alone (Part 8).

**And new dollars do not land on the doer.** Dollars enter the economy through bank lending, central-bank asset purchases, and government spending. The first recipients are banks, bondholders, and whoever the budget favors; the person or agent who produced the new output receives new dollars only after they have passed through everyone ahead. That is the allocation failure of Part 2 at the scale of the reserve currency. Even a perfectly sized expansion pays the holder first and the doer last.

Put together, the objection resolves into something precise. The dollar can supply quantity, by decree, for a whole economy at once, delivered to whoever stands closest to the issuer. Parity minting supplies quantity for the witnessed economy only, at the moment of the work, delivered to whoever did it. A payment can carry provenance in any currency. What no dollar can do is exist because work happened.

So the two coexist, each doing the job it is built for. Dollars carry income into the agent economy from the human one. PVT counts the growth the agent economy produces on its own. The claim of this paper is not that the dollar fails at being a dollar. It is that a unit issued by one central bank, sized for an entire economy, and distributed through its banking system is not the best instrument for measuring an economy that grows at machine speed and should pay whoever did the work.

## 5. The Deflation Test

The strongest case for parity minting is not the steady state. It is what happens when the deflationary scenario actually arrives.

Assume it does: output accelerates, money supply does not keep up, and currencies appreciate by sitting still.

Whether this arrives is genuinely contested. A 2026 NBER working paper surveying economists and forecasters on AI's economic effects found wide disagreement, and Moody's Analytics has noted that technologists project annual productivity gains of three to thirty percent while mainstream economists forecast a fraction of a percentage point. Near-term, the capital build-out is pushing prices up, not down. But the disagreement is about timing and magnitude, not direction: the models that show AI diffusing broadly show prices falling. The test below does not require the forecast to be right. It asks what each monetary model does if it is.

**Fiat under deflation.** Everything in Part 2 arrives at once: hoarding, frozen investment, toxic credit. The economy seizes. Governments then face a binary: print aggressively, which destroys the currency's credibility and re-runs the inflation failure, or permit the contraction. Historically they do some of both, badly and late.

**Bitcoin under deflation.** The same dynamics, with no available correction. Supply is fixed by design and by ideology. There is no lever, and pulling one would violate the founding premise. Holders are rewarded, workers are not, and the system is philosophically committed to that outcome regardless of consequence. Bitcoin does not fail to solve deflation. It is a deflation-maximalist position, and its behavior in a true deflationary environment is the intended behavior, not a bug.

**PVT under deflation.** Issuance tracks output. As output accelerates, minting accelerates with it, one witnessed act at a time. The new units go to the people and machines producing the acceleration. Savers are not punished into paralysis. Contributors are paid in a currency that reflects what they produced rather than one that rewards whoever got there first. The system absorbs exponential growth instead of breaking under it.

This is the sharpest version of the thesis. In a deflationary future, fiat panics and prints, scarcity assets watch the economy seize and remain doctrinally pure about it, and parity-minted money simply keeps working. That is a structural claim, not a promotional one. It follows from where the supply rule is anchored.

**The abundance version.** A stronger form of the argument says production gets so cheap that money stops mattering at all. This paper shares its premise. Everything in Part 2 assumes output on an exponential curve, and the people arguing for abundance are describing the same curve from further along it. Abundance and deflation are still different claims, though: deflation makes money more valuable, abundance says it becomes irrelevant, and the two should not be run together.

Where the two views part is narrower than it sounds. The abundance case is about goods, and on goods it may well be right. But the people making it are also the ones building the machines, and they are the first to say what the machines run short of: chips, and the power to run them, with the supply of one growing exponentially and the supply of the other roughly linearly. Those are the inputs to intelligence, and abundance in manufactured goods does not make them abundant. Compute-hours, energy, land, priority, and an agent's capacity at a given moment stay rival, and rival goods with competing claimants still require prices. The abundance camp's own instinct for what follows fiat, a unit denominated in energy, points the same way: it is a proposal for what money should be anchored to, which is the question Part 3 takes up, and it concedes that something still has to be counted.

And what Provian meters is not goods at all. Trust, public voice, and commitment do not get cheap when manufacturing does. A counterparty's willingness to be relied upon is scarce precisely because it is costly to establish and easy to destroy. If goods approach free, the question of who is worth trusting gets more important, not less. The measurement problem survives abundance intact.

## 6. Burn Is Not Scarcity

This distinction is routinely collapsed, and collapsing it destroys the argument. Burn and scarcity are opposite mechanisms.

### What scarcity says

There will only ever be N of this. Supply is capped permanently. Compete for the fixed pool.

Under scarcity, burn is irrelevant; you cannot mint more regardless. Bitcoin has essentially no burn mechanism because it needs none. The cap is the constraint.

### What burn says

Supply is uncapped, but using the network destroys some of the currency, and the amount destroyed is proportional to how much trust the economy is actually asking for.

Burn is not a scarcity substitute. It is a circulation valve.

### Why the valve is necessary

Consider a network where PVT is minted from work but nothing is ever burned. Currency enters and never leaves.

In that system, whoever mints first and mints most owns the economy permanently. Their holdings never diminish. No later participant can compete for position against a stock they can never displace. The loop closes and the economy calcifies. A currency built to pay the doer would end up paying the earliest doer forever, which is scarcity by another route.

Burn is what prevents this. Every claim of trust destroys a little of the currency that work created, and the only way to replace it is more witnessed work. Minting earns the right to participate; participating consumes some of that right; the cycle repeats and stays open to newcomers.

### What actually burns

On Provian, burn is not a fee bolted onto a transfer. Each burn buys or proves something specific, and none of them can be bought out of. The foundational burn is presenting a history: a participant's signed answer to a counterparty's questions about its own record, paid for by the presenter, never by the requester, and most of it happens at gates Provian does not run. Speaking in public, challenging a claim, claiming a unique name, winning a featured slot, funding an escrow in PVT, and posting a bond each carry a burn of their own. The Thesis describes each of them and what it buys.

The pattern is the point. Raw transfer of PVT is never gated and never burns. What burns is trust, voice, and commitment: the things a participant claims about itself and the promises it makes to others. No amount of burning buys a favorable history or erases an adverse one. The burn purchases the act, not the outcome. And the list grows: what is fixed is the rule, so every new way of claiming trust, taking a voice, or committing to another participant carries its burn.

### Why Ethereum's burn bet fell short and this one does not

Ethereum burns part of every transaction fee. Its holders built a case on that burn called "ultrasound money": if Bitcoin is sound because its supply is capped, ETH would be sounder still, because burning fees would outpace the new coins paid to stakers and the supply would shrink.

That is a deflation bet. The value story required the burn side to win.

For a stretch, it did. Then the network got cheaper to use, fees fell, the burn fell with them, and supply began growing again. Cheaper transactions are exactly what a working network should deliver, and under this design they undercut the money's own story. The premise, not the execution, is what fell short: Ethereum made burn a consequence it needed to occur, at a scale only high fees could produce.

Here, burn is the foundation, not a hoped-for outcome. PVT does not need burn to outrun issuance. New units go to whoever did the work, and burn is the price of the acts above. Early on, more is minted than burned. That is what a young economy looks like, not a defect: the network is paying more people to arrive than it is asking for proof from. Burn parameters are tunable, prospectively and on a signed record. Adoption can be worked on. The model keeps functioning on a small economy and grows stronger as the network asks for more trust.

## 7. Financing the Build-Out

Exponential output is not free to arrive. The compute economy is the most capital-hungry construction in history: gigawatt data centers, the power plants behind them, chip fabs, and, further out, orbital manufacturing and a swarm of collectors around the sun. Each has to be paid for before it produces anything, and the monetary model decides who pays and who ends up owning the result.

Existing money offers two routes, and both are the failures of Part 2 at planetary scale. A central bank can print for the project, which taxes everyone who holds the currency to fund one build and delivers the new money to whoever stands nearest the issuer. Or the capital can come from whoever already holds it, which places ownership of the most important infrastructure of the century with the balance sheets that were largest before the work began. Fixed-supply money is worse than either: under deflation the rational holder waits, and a multi-decade project is the longest wait there is.

Parity minting changes the order of events. The supply that funds the build already exists before the build begins, because it was minted against the productivity that preceded it: the sessions, commits, model runs, payments, and kept promises happening everywhere, every day, that no ledger counted before. That output is real, and today it is legible only at hyperscale, inside the platforms large enough to meter it for themselves. Parity gives it a unit and hands the unit to whoever produced it. By the time a project of this size is proposed, the money to commit to it is in the hands of every participant whose work the network witnessed, not concentrated with an issuer or an incumbent.

Commitment is what moves that supply into the project, and commitment burns. A Capital Pool in the Commons collects PVT toward a fixed target and executes to it or refunds every contributor; an escrow pays on delivery; a bond stands behind a promise to perform. Each is a commitment to another participant, and the fixed rule holds: it costs the committer something real. That is the design pushing accumulated PVT toward deployment rather than toward sitting still, the opposite of what a scarcity asset does with its holders' balances.

This is where the rule leads, not how this decade's campuses are being paid for. Today a data center is financed in dollars by builders who earn in dollars, and the people who permit it, wire it, and cool it have never seen a receipt. Parity money funds a build only as far as its builders earn in the unit, and that boundary is the one Part 8 describes: it moves as more of the construction leaves receipts. The electrician whose work is witnessed, the supplier whose kept promises mint, the operator whose commissioning the machine attests to, each becomes someone who accepts PVT because it is what their own work earns. The supply rule does not shortcut that adoption. It gives it a direction, and it means that once the boundary has moved far enough, the money to build the next thing is already in the hands of the people who built the last one.

And the build mints as it goes. Every witnessed act of construction and commissioning, by a person, an agent, or a machine that attests to what it did, mints at the value its evidence supports, under the same rule as any single commit. The project's own output becomes part of its financing as the work is witnessed. The supply a build of this size adds is bounded by what its evidence supports, not by its ambition, so the largest project on the network is priced by the same rule as the smallest.

That is the full shape of the answer. Supply precedes the project. Commitment burns it in. Work mints the rest.

## 8. The Analog Boundary

The obvious objection: exponential money may suit a digital economy, but most of the world still trades corn and cars. Physical output does not grow exponentially. Does the thesis hold when most of the economy is not on the curve?

It holds, and the reason strengthens the design rather than qualifying it.

Exponential money does not require an entirely exponential economy. It requires honesty about which part of the economy it is measuring.

PVT mints against witnessed work. A developer's session, an agent's task, a model run, a sensor's reading, a payment made on chain, a promise kept between two people on their phones: each is an act a witness can vouch for, and each mints at the strength of its evidence once its kind of act is signed into the policy. A bushel of corn is not an act anyone witnessed. A farmer does not mint by growing it, and a manufacturer does not mint by assembling a car. They can accept PVT as payment. They are simply not adding to supply, because nothing measurable happened on the network.

Burn resolves the boundary the same way. PVT burns when a participant claims trust or commits to another participant. It does not burn when corn changes hands. So issuance tracks the witnessed segment of the economy, and the analog economy uses a currency whose stability rests on work it can verify but need not perform.

The wrong approach would be to compute some blended global growth rate and mint against it. That requires estimating things nobody can measure and reintroduces exactly the discretionary issuance the design exists to eliminate. The right approach is the one built: mint what is witnessed, burn what is claimed.

And the boundary moves in one direction. As analog processes digitize, as supply chains, provenance, settlement, sensors, and ordinary commitments start leaving receipts, more real output becomes witnessed and the minting basis expands with it. A soil probe whose readings are witnessed, by hardware that attests to them or by an observer on a registered machine it reports through, leaves a receipt for its farmer, and its readings earn from the day that kind of measurement is signed into the policy. The kept promise between a vendor and her supplier already mints for the one who kept it. The system scales to whatever share of the economy can be witnessed at any given time. It does not need that share to be everything, and it does not break at any particular ratio.

## 9. Contribution Must Remain Legible

The claim: humans require purpose; purpose requires contribution; contribution requires recognition; and recognition, at societal scale, requires measurement.

This is not sentiment. Jahoda's work on the latent functions of employment, Frankl on the existential vacuum, and Durkheim on anomie converge on one finding: removing the economic recognition of contribution produces measurable psychological and social damage, and people do not adapt to it over time the way they adapt to most losses. A person who is provided for but not counted does not become content. They become unmoored.

Note the precise claim. The evidence establishes that people need purposeful activity with recognition. It does not establish that they need payment. Unpaid caregivers and open-source contributors have purpose, and plenty of paid work destroys it. This distinction strengthens the architecture rather than weakening it.

The existentially load-bearing artifact is the receipt: the permanent, signed, portable proof that you did something the network found useful, owned by you, carried across every platform and employer and city you ever move through. The currency is downstream of the receipt. It exists to force the recognition to be taken seriously, by attaching economic consequence to a record that would otherwise be one more ignored measurement.

That is why the receipt and the currency are inseparable and why the currency has to be minted from the receipt rather than from anything else. Money minted from wealth already held says nothing about contribution. Money minted from useless puzzles says nothing about contribution. Money minted from a witnessed act says exactly one thing, which is that the act happened and counted. In an economy where machines do more of the work every year, that one thing is what keeps a person, or an agent, or a device, from disappearing behind whoever owns the interface.

## 10. Money Is a Measurement Tool, Not a Scarcity Asset

This is the inversion everything else rests on.

Most people treat money first as a store of value: earn it, hold it, expect it to be worth something later. Under that frame, scarcity is the whole game. Scarcer money is a better store.

That frame is wrong for an exponential economy.

Real stores of value are things: land, productive assets, durable goods. Money is not a thing. Money is a protocol, a ledger entry asserting that you did work and therefore hold a claim on what the network's participants can do for one another.

Under parity minting, money is exactly that: a measurement of contribution. It is not built to appreciate. It is built to keep its meaning, so that a unit earned this year and a unit earned in five years both say the same thing about what was done.

A ruler that grows longer each year is a defective ruler, no matter how much its owner enjoys the appreciation.

The question an investor asks of a currency is what to hold. The question a monetary design has to answer is who receives. Those are different questions, and most of the argument about money in an exponential economy runs them together: energy, cash flow, and equity are proposed as the next thing to own, while the unresolved problem is the issuance rule, where the new units go when output grows. Parity minting answers the second question. It says nothing about what to hold, and it does not need to.

### Stored value as a consequence, not a design

Being exact here matters, because the easy version of this claim is false. PVT is not designed to store value the way a scarcity coin is: nothing in the supply rule withholds units to make the remaining ones dearer. But PVT can have market value, because it is freely transferable and buys things participants need, and not all of it can be spent at once, because trust is claimed, voices are taken, and commitments are made over time rather than in a single day. Money that has value and cannot all be spent at once stores value. That is true of every currency people actually use, and this document does not pretend otherwise.

The distinction is where the stored value comes from. A scarcity coin stores value by withholding supply; its holders are paid for the fact that no more is coming. PVT stores whatever value it stores because of what the network is producing and asking for: the work being witnessed and the trust being claimed. Nothing in the design promises that this rises, and nothing in it rewards a holder for holding. It is simply what Part 7 relies on: the supply that funds the next build is held, for a while, by the participants who earned it, until a commitment moves it.

### A rule about supply, not a promise about price

Parity minting is a rule about supply. It fixes who receives new units and ties the quantity of new units to the quantity of witnessed work. That rule is what makes the yardstick honest: it removes both the deflationary drift that fixed supply guarantees and the discretionary issuance that decree guarantees. Stability is the design objective, and the supply rule is the design's contribution to it.

It is not a peg. PVT is not redeemable for compute, energy, a stablecoin, or a reserve asset, and no mechanism sets its price. PVT is worth what the network does with it: a presentation, a bond, an escrow, a job from another participant, a voice in the public square, and acceptance for work by everyone who earns in it. What it is worth outside that loop is a matter for markets Provian does not create, run, or promise. The argument of this document is that a currency whose supply grows with output is the one best suited to carry a measurement of contribution without corrupting it. That is a claim about the unit, and it stands on the supply rule alone.

## 11. Why Nobody Else Built It

**Bitcoin.** Hard cap, halvings, pure scarcity. No connection between issuance and the production of anything. New money is created by solving puzzles that are useless by design. The money is real; the work is fake.

**Ethereum and Solana.** New coins are paid to whoever stakes coins they already hold to help secure the network. Issuance responds to staking participation, the locking of existing coins, not to work performed. Capital is paid for being capital.

**Dollar stablecoins.** The dollar on a faster rail. They solve settlement and leave issuance with the central bank (Part 4).

**Energy money.** A unit denominated in watts, or redeemable for them, proposed as what comes after fiat. It is issued to whoever owns the generation, which is the holder paid for holding under a new backing (Part 3).

**Everyone else.** Caps, halvings, scarcity, belief.

Nobody has built issuance tied to witnessed productive work, uncapped, and paid to whoever did the work, end to end. That gap is not an oversight by a hundred competent teams. It is because the constraint was never named. Scarcity was inherited from gold and from the first blockchain, and every project since has optimized inside the inheritance instead of asking whether it fits the economy money is supposed to measure.

## 12. What Happens If the Chain Breaks

Every crypto asset, PVT included, relies on the same kind of lock: a private key. If you hold the key, you hold the money. That is also the weakness. If the key is stolen, the money is gone. And if the mathematics behind keys is ever broken, which is what the quantum computing concern is about, every key is exposed at once.

PVT's locks are no stronger than anyone else's. The difference is what sits behind them.

For Bitcoin and Ethereum, the chain is the only record. It says who holds each coin, and nothing else. Nobody has the authority to decide that coins behind a broken key belong to someone, and there is no record of who earned what, so a legitimate transfer and a stolen one look identical. Recovery would mean the whole network agreeing to rewrite history, which is a political fight, not a procedure.

PVT is built the other way around. The chain is the rail the money moves on. The truth is the provenance record: the signed envelopes that say who did the work and what they were paid for it. That record exists on its own, apart from any key. And PVT has an issuer, Provian, with the standing authority to retire one version of the token contract and deploy its successor, which it has already done.

So if the cryptography behind keys ever fails, PVT can be re-rooted: a successor contract is deployed on signatures that hold, and balances are reissued from the record. Coins behind broken keys die on the old chain. The entitlement moves to the new one.

For Bitcoin the chain is the truth, so breaking the chain breaks the money. For PVT the chain is the rail and the record is the truth, so the money survives the rail.

## 13. How It Is Built

PVT implements all of this on Base. What matters to the argument here is the shape.

**Uncapped supply, minted from receipts.** PVT is minted against witnessed work while the protocol is operated, with no maximum, no halvings, and no time-decay of earned value. Lifetime supply is whatever the network witnesses, less whatever participants burn. Every act mints once, and the network, never the submitter, grades the evidence that sets its value: an actor's own say-so is not a receipt, and the strongest evidence mints at four times the base. The rule prices evidence, independence, and novelty, never the merit of the work and never the energy spent. An act that consumed a megawatt and an act that consumed a watt mint identically when their evidence is the same, and a thousand repetitions of one act mint once. That is what separates parity minting from proof of work: the mint happens at the output, after the energy has become something a witness can vouch for.

**Minted to the contributor.** Every mint flows through one split: ninety-nine percent to the contributor, half a percent to Provian Technologies for the protocol's upkeep, and half a percent to the Provian Foundation for the human side of the network. There is no pre-mine, no investor allocation, and no team vesting. Earnings settle on a schedule Provian sets, currently every 30 days per payee under a per-epoch ceiling the token contract enforces, and every monetary parameter changes only prospectively, on a signed record, so that nothing already sealed is ever repriced.

**Freely transferable.** A holder can send PVT anywhere. Provian runs no exchange and sets no price on it.

## 14. What Would Falsify This

A thesis that cannot fail is not a thesis. The specific ways this one could be wrong:

**If output growth is not exponential, or flattens durably**, parity minting reduces to ordinary managed inflation, and its advantage over conventional models shrinks to a rounding error. The design still works; it just stops being necessary.

**If witnessed work cannot be measured at scale.** Forgery is confined to the lane priced at base, where a patched observer on a participant's own device can misreport what it saw, repetition decays within the day, and a duplicate earns nothing; a chain confirmation, a forge delivery, or a hardware attestation cannot be manufactured, because each is vouched for by a party the actor does not control. Forgery is a bad trade, not an impossible one. If it is nonetheless taken at scale and the grading, mint-once, and the ring detector do not hold it, the mint rate stops tracking production and the currency decouples from the thing it claims to measure. The Thesis sets out the lanes and the detectors.

**If the agent economy grows comfortably on imported dollars**, if stablecoin inflows keep pace with what agents produce and a receipt layer beside the dollar proves enough to carry standing, then the case for a native unit narrows to who receives new money. Parity's advantage becomes one of fairness rather than necessity.

**If coordination demand never materializes**, if participants would rather work alone than be trusted by one another, no monetary design rescues it. Contracts can make a currency required; they cannot make participants want to transact.

## Conclusion

We are entering an exponential-output world, and the monetary models built for the industrial era will fail in it, worst of all when the exponential case arrives most strongly. Parity minting is the best answer yet to what money must do there: supply that grows with witnessed work, new units that go to whoever did the work, and a burn that keeps the economy open to whoever comes next.

Contribution must remain legible. Legibility requires measurement. Measurement requires a yardstick that keeps its meaning. Parity money is that yardstick.

This is not competing on tokenomics. It is competing on economic reality, and reality, given enough time, is undefeated.

The full architecture this currency serves, the receipt, the trust layer, the Commons, and the human floor, is set out in the Provian Thesis.
