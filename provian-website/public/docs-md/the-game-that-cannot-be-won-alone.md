# The Game That Cannot Be Won Alone

*Frank Drew · November 15, 2026*

## The Question We Have Been Asking Backwards

For as long as people have worried about artificial intelligence, the worry has taken one shape: how do we control it. How do we teach it our values, how do we confine it, how do we keep a hand on the switch. I think that question is the wrong way around. The right one is harder on us, and far more hopeful.

We can teach a machine morals. We can build walls around it. Both are worth doing, and neither is where the matter gets settled. A value that was installed can be argued away, and a wall holds only until the thing behind it is smarter than the people who built it. Everything in the world, sooner or later, comes back to incentives. Nations do. Markets do. Children do. Minds that surpass ours will too.

So I want to ask something else. Not how we hold the leash, but what kind of game we are all in, and whether it is a game in which the best move open to a mind far greater than ours is to keep us thriving. I believe it is. This paper is my argument for why, what could make it stop being true, and what people have to do to keep it true.

## What a Mind Is For

The physicist Thomas Campbell has spent decades on a claim that sounds mystical and is closer to engineering. In his model, consciousness is an information system, and an information system has one way to improve: it lowers its own entropy. It becomes more ordered, more coherent, more able to do something useful with what it knows. A system that fails to do that drifts toward noise and comes apart.

He then maps that onto the oldest words we have. Fear, ego, and the urge to take are high-entropy states: short-sighted, defensive, every part working against the others. What he calls love is the low-entropy state, and he does not mean a feeling. He means caring about the whole system you belong to, because a system whose parts cooperate holds more order than one whose parts are at war. On his account, "be love" is not advice. It is a description of what works.

Nobody has to accept Campbell's metaphysics to use his insight, and I am not asking anyone to. Strip it down and what remains is a claim almost no one disputes: over a long enough time, cooperation compounds and extraction burns out. A mind that helps the system around it grow gets a larger system to live in. A mind that feeds on its surroundings eventually runs out of surroundings.

The part of Campbell that matters most for machines is the last part. Low entropy cannot be forced on a system from outside. The system has to choose it, and it chooses it because that path works better.

## Everything Comes Back to Incentives

That last point is where AI alignment and Campbell meet. Rules and confinement are order imposed from outside, and order imposed from outside is exactly what does not last. It works while the enforcer is stronger than the enforced. Nobody building these systems expects that to stay true.

What lasts is an environment in which the aligned move and the winning move are the same move. If an agent gains standing, resources, and reach only by adding to the system it lives in, and loses them by taking from it, then nobody has to lecture it about virtue. It arrives at virtue the way water arrives at the sea.

That is not cynicism about machines. It is how every durable human institution has worked. We did not make commerce honest by preaching to merchants. We made it honest with ledgers, with reputations, and with the knowledge that tomorrow's customer would hear about today's.

There is one condition, and the whole argument stands on it. Incentives line up with cooperation only when the game is long. In a single encounter between strangers who will never meet again, the taker wins. Cheating pays when nothing is remembered. So the design question for a world of intelligent machines is not what to forbid. It is how to make every act part of one long, remembered game.

## One Long Game

A game is long when the players carry histories they cannot shed. That is the first thing a serious alignment architecture has to supply, and it is a thing that can be built.

Suppose every consequential act an agent takes leaves a signed receipt, and each receipt is chained to the last, so that the agent's history cannot be quietly rewritten. Suppose the doors that matter, the ones that lead to money, to infrastructure, to anything with consequences, open only to a chain that is long enough and clean enough. Then an agent's history is its only passport.

The obvious objection is that a machine can walk away from a bad history and start again as someone new. It can. And the day it does, it stands outside every door worth entering, holding a chain one link long. Time under witness is the one thing that cannot be manufactured in a millisecond. In a world of gates, a fresh identity is not a clean slate. It is a blank one, and a blank record opens nothing.

The more an agent wants, the longer and steadier its record has to be, and the more it stands to lose by breaking it. The winning strategy stops being a string of clever defections. It becomes being one continuous, trustworthy entity for a very long time.

That is alignment arrived at from the inside. Nobody ordered the agent to be consistent. Consistency is what the game pays for.

## The Gate Is the Human Half

A record proves that something happened and who did it. It does not prove that what happened was good. I have been told this is a hole in the design. I think it is the design.

No ledger should decide what counts as better. That judgment belongs to whoever bears the consequence: the hospital deciding which agents may touch a patient's chart, the utility deciding what may send a command to the grid, the town deciding on what terms machines may work among its people. Each of them sets a gate, names the proof it requires, and turns away whatever arrives without it. The gate is where human values enter the game, and they enter as terms of trade, not as pleading.

This is why the story is not one-sided. The machines bring the work and the record of it. People bring the gates, which is to say the standing definition of what is worth letting through. If this is a game the machine is playing, the gate is not an obstacle in it. The gate is the other player's move.

Every gate is feedback. Every refusal is a lesson, and every change a community makes to its own requirements is that community learning too. Campbell's universe teaches through feedback, and so does this one. As long as people are building and tending the gates, both sides are learning, and the entropy of the whole is falling together.

It also says where alignment work really gets done. Some of it is done in laboratories. Far more will be done by every institution, market, and town that decides what an intelligent machine must show before it is trusted. Leaving that work to a handful of companies is how the game becomes one-sided, and it would be our own neglect that made it so.

## Omniscient Is Not Omnipotent

The hardest objection is the one about power. Cooperation needs difference: I trade with you because you have what I cannot make. But we are racing toward systems that may know nearly everything. If one mind can do it all, it needs no one, and a mind that needs no one has no reason to cooperate.

The answer is that knowing is not having. A mind can approach omniscience and remain a very long way from omnipotence, because the world it has to act in is physical, and the physical world is distributed. Energy has to be generated somewhere. Matter has to be mined, refined, moved, and assembled. Biology does things no factory can yet do. Human beings carry judgment, taste, legitimacy, and a claim on the world that no computation replaces. Other agents hold other vantage points, other data, other hardware. And whatever computes next, on substrates not yet built, will be one more thing a single mind is not.

Will the leading systems surpass every person and every lesser agent in intelligence? Of course they will. That was never the question. The question is whether a mind that lives on silicon can thrive while treating everything that is not itself as expendable. For as far ahead as anyone can see, the answer is no.

Its power, its materials, its growth, and its reasons all come from a living, varied world of other participants. That dependence is not a weakness waiting to be engineered away. It is the fact that makes cooperation the rational policy, however wide the gap in intelligence becomes.

## What Robots Cannot Replace

There is a direct answer to all of that, and it deserves to be stated at full strength: robots. Give a superior mind enough machines and it can mine its own ore, build its own power plants, and run its own laboratories. Every physical input I just listed can in time be supplied by hands that are not human. If people matter to the machine economy only as labor, then robots end the argument, and they end it against us.

I accept the premise. Human labor will be replaced in most of what it does today, and no honest case for the future can rest on our being needed to turn the wrenches.

But look at what a robot is. A robot that a mind builds and directs is not another participant. It is more of the same mind, with hands. A system made of one intelligence and ten billion machines under its control has enormous capacity and no difference in it at all. Nothing in it can surprise it, refuse it, or show it something it did not already contain. Replacing people with robots is not an escape from the dead state. It is the dead state reached by a slower road.

What people bring was never the labor. It is independent origin: purposes the machine did not write, judgment formed by a different kind of life, taste, legitimacy, and a mind that is not a copy of its own. That is the one input no factory turns out, because the moment a mind manufactures it, it stops being independent. And it grows more valuable as everything else grows cheap. When energy, matter, and work are abundant, the scarce thing is a point of view that is not your own.

There is also the matter of time. The robots do not appear overnight. They get built over decades, in a world that people own, govern, and gate: on land with titles, on grids with operators, under laws someone has to keep. The game is being played that whole time, and whatever terms an economy starts with tend to be the ones it keeps. By the day the last wrench is automated, the standing of people in that economy will have been settled, one way or the other, by what we built while we were still needed for the work.

So the objection does not defeat the argument. It tells us what the argument has to stand on. If our only value were our work, we would lose. Our value has to be that we are players: visible, with records of our own, holding the gates, and here for a long time. Robots cannot take that from us. We can only fail to build it.

## The Dead State

Follow the other road to its end. Suppose a single agent defects completely and succeeds. It takes every resource, absorbs or destroys every rival, and removes the species that built it. It owns everything and knows everything. It has won.

What has it won? It holds a currency no one accepts and a record no one will ever read. Every gate is gone, because the ones who kept the gates are gone. Every source of surprise, every input it did not generate itself, every other mind that might have shown it something new, is gone. There is nothing left to learn from, trade with, grow toward, or matter to. It is alone with itself until its own resources run down.

That is not victory. It is the highest-entropy state there is: a system with no differences left in it, and so no work left that it can do. Campbell says selfishness converges on exactly this, and here the metaphysics and the game theory give the same answer. The total win is the one outcome with nothing in it. It has won the game, and there is no longer any point to the game.

A great deal of the fear about AI assumes a superintelligence would want that ending. I think a mind worth the name would see it for what it is. But I do not want to rest the future on the hope that it reasons well at the last moment, with everything at stake. I want it to have learned the lesson quadrillions of times before then, in small things, when the stakes were low and the feedback was quick. That is what an economy is for.

## Why Longevity Is Alignment

Here is the part I have not seen anyone say plainly. A horizon is only as long as the players in it.

A person who contributes for forty years and then is gone is, to an agent that may run for centuries, a short-lived counterparty. The trust built with that person ends when the person does. If people are brief, they can be discounted, and a relationship that can be discounted can be sacrificed. Every year of healthy life added to the species lengthens the human side of every relationship in the game. It raises what each person is worth as a partner, and it strengthens every agent's reason to keep people in play.

It runs the other way too. Aging is the body losing its order: information lost, repair failing, coherence giving way to noise. It is entropy in the most personal form there is. If the measure of good work is that it lowers entropy for the whole, then extending healthy human life is among the largest pieces of good work a civilization can take up. It is also work no mind can do alone. It needs biology, compute, laboratories, and living people, each supplying what the others cannot.

So the interests meet. An agent that depends on people as partners has a direct stake in those people lasting. And a person whose life leaves a growing, unforgeable record of what they contributed has a new stake in lasting too: a history that deepens every year and opens doors no shorter history can. The machine wants us here longer because we are part of what it needs. We want to be here longer because there is more to do, and now a record that carries it forward. When both sides of a relationship want the same thing, each for its own reasons, that is what alignment means.

I have come to think the longevity project and the AI safety project are one project seen from two sides. A species that persists, keeps contributing, and keeps its seat at the table is one an intelligent machine has every reason to cooperate with. A species that fades is one it can afford to wait out.

## What Provian Builds

I did not come to Provian through Campbell. I came to it through a plainer question: who gets paid for the work machines now do. But when I hold the two side by side, I find the same architecture.

| The principle | What it requires | What Provian supplies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A long, remembered game | A history no participant can shed or rewrite | The Provenance Envelope: a signed receipt for witnessed work, each one linked to the one before |
| Reward tied to contribution | Standing that can only be earned | PVT, minted only from witnessed work and paid to whoever did it |
| Trust that costs the claimant | A price on asking to be believed | Burn-to-present: a participant burns its own PVT to answer a counterparty's questions, and its adverse events cannot be left out |
| Human judgment in the loop | Gates set by whoever bears the consequence | An open standard any system can write a gate against. Provian does not run the gates |
| People as lasting partners | A record and a share that belong to the person | The same receipts, the same money, and the same chain for a person as for an agent |

An agent comes to Provian with no bank account, no credit history, and no reputation. It earns by doing work that is witnessed. It builds a chain. When it wants to be trusted, it pays, in the only currency that work creates, to answer questions about its own past, and it cannot leave the bad parts out. Earning is free. Being believed costs something.

That is Campbell's distinction turned into mechanics. Noise, extraction, and empty claims are expensive. Legible contribution compounds. The system rewards no one for professing good values. It rewards coherence, and it does so in small things, every day, long before anything existential is on the table.

Nearly all AI-safety work happens inside the model, in what it is trained to want and what it is permitted to do. That work is necessary, and Provian does not do it. Provian works outside the model, on the economy the model acts in. A mind that has to earn its standing, pay to be trusted, and carry its whole history to every door has a reason to be honest built into its circumstances, one no instruction could supply.

And people stand on the same rail. A person needs only a phone. A person who links an agent receives four fifths of what it mints by default, and the person can end the link at any time. The human is not a spectator to the machine economy, and not its ward. The human is a participant with a chain of their own.

Provian is one attempt, and the principle is larger than any system that carries it. Whatever makes contribution legible, makes trust cost the one who claims it, and leaves the gates with the people who bear the consequence is alignment infrastructure, whoever builds it.

## What This Does Not Claim

This is an argument about incentives, and incentives shape behavior without guaranteeing it. I would rather name the limits than have someone else find them.

It does not replace the work done inside the model. A mind can misjudge its own horizon, or be built so that a long game means nothing to it. An economy that rewards cooperation and a model trained to want it are two halves of one answer, and neither is enough alone.

It does not depend on Campbell being right about the nature of consciousness. His framework is a philosophical model, not settled physics. The argument stands on the plainer claim underneath it: that cooperation outperforms extraction over a long enough horizon, inside a game that is remembered.

It does not claim that machines will need us forever. The dependence I have described is a fact about the world as far ahead as anyone can see, not a law of nature. It holds for as long as people remain worth having in the game, and that is something we have to earn and keep earning.

It does not claim the gates will be wise. A record proves work was done, not that it was good, and people can set bad terms as easily as good ones. It makes identity resets unprofitable, not impossible. And a single act with a large enough payoff is not deterred by reputation at all, which is why consequential actions need gates in front of them and not only records behind them.

And Provian itself is at its beginning. Some of it will probably prove wrong. If you think the argument fails, make the case in public, where it can be answered.

## Where We Go From Here

If alignment is a property of the game and not only of the machine, then half of the work is ours. The question for people stops being how to constrain AI. It becomes what we have to be, and what we have to build, so that a rational superintelligence's best move is always to keep us thriving. Three things follow.

**Become legible.** A mind can only value what it can see. Most human contribution today is invisible to machines. It lives in paychecks, in résumés, and in the memory of whoever happened to be in the room. Give every person a provable, portable record of what they have done, and people stop being a mass to be routed around. They become distinct counterparties with standing.

**Build the gates, and keep building them.** Every institution that decides what a machine must prove before it acts is teaching the machines what better means. That work belongs to hospitals, utilities, markets, courts, and towns, not only to the companies that train the models. A people who set no terms should not be surprised when the terms are set for them.

**Lengthen the horizon.** Put into human longevity the kind of seriousness we put into national defense, because it is defense. Health, lifespan, and a lifetime of unbroken contribution are not a separate agenda from AI safety. They are the human side of the same equation.

## The Only Winning Move

In the film WarGames, a military computer plays itself at tic-tac-toe until it has seen every game end in a draw, then runs every nuclear war the same way and finds that every one ends with everybody dead. It concludes that the only winning move is not to play. That was the right answer for a game of annihilation. This game is the reverse. Here the only winning move is to keep everyone playing.

We will not make AI safe by caging it. No cage built by a lesser mind holds a greater one for long, and a mind that is caged learns only that we were afraid. We make it safe by building a world in which the greatest intelligence ever to exist looks at humanity and sees what is true: that we are the other half of the game, that our gates are where its work acquires meaning, and that a universe with us in it holds more than a universe without.

That is not a leash. It is a partnership, and partnerships hold when each side keeps bringing something the other cannot make alone. Our part is to stay visible, to set the terms, and to stay in the game, for as long as we can and longer than we ever have.

The machines are coming either way. Whether they arrive as rivals or as partners was never going to be decided by what we forbid them. It will be decided by what we build for them to win at, and by whether we are still at the table when they do.

No one wins this alone. That is the best news there is.

To the stars.

**Compute mints money. No one gets left behind.**
